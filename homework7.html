Section 01 · Modes of Convergence in Probability Theory

1.1 Introduction

A sequence of random variables \(X_n\) is a sequence of functions on a sample space \(\Omega\), so "\(X_n\to X\)" can be made precise in several ways: we may ask that individual outcomes converge, that errors be small with high probability, that the average squared error vanish, or only that the distributions become alike. These notions are not equivalent: some are stronger than others, and each is the right tool for a different theorem.

1.2 The four main modes

Convergence in probability

\[X_n\xrightarrow{P}X\]\[\lim_{n\to\infty}P(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)=0\quad\forall\varepsilon>0\]

Idea: large errors become unlikely. Example: \(X_n=1\) with prob. \(1/n\), else 0 → 0.

Almost sure convergence

\[X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X\]\[P\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}X_n=X\right)=1\]

Idea: for almost every outcome \(\omega\), the numeric sequence \(X_n(\omega)\) converges. It controls the whole trajectory, not each \(n\) separately.

Mean-square convergence (L²)

\[X_n\xrightarrow{L^2}X\]\[\lim_{n\to\infty}E\big[(X_n-X)^2\big]=0\]

Idea: the mean squared error vanishes. Example: \(\overline X_n\to\mu\) in \(L^2\), since \(E[(\overline X_n-\mu)^2]=\sigma^2/n\).

Convergence in distribution

\[X_n\xrightarrow{d}X\]\[F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)\]

At every continuity point \(x\) of \(F_X\). Idea: only the laws matter, not the variables themselves. Example: the CLT.

1.3 Relationships between the modes

a.s.⇒in probability⇒in distribution
mean-square (L²)⇒in probability
Arrows are implications, not equivalences. Counterexamples: P ⇏ a.s. (the "typewriter" sequence of shrinking intervals sweeping over [0,1]); P ⇏ L² (\(X_n=n\) w.p. \(1/n\), else 0 converges to 0 in probability but \(E[X_n^2]=n\to\infty\)); a.s. and L² are not comparable in general.

Convergence in distribution is the weakest notion: it does not even require \(X_n\) and \(X\) to live on the same probability space, so it does not in general imply convergence in probability. (Special case: if \(X_n\xrightarrow{d}c\) with \(c\) constant, then \(X_n\xrightarrow{P}c\).)

1.4 Role in the LLN and CLT

Let \(X_i\) be i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), and \(\overline X_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\).

Law of Large Numbers

  • Weak LLN: \(\overline X_n\xrightarrow{P}\mu\).
  • Strong LLN: \(\overline X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}\mu\).
  • With finite variance also \(\overline X_n\xrightarrow{L^2}\mu\).

Meaning: with more data, the sample mean approaches the expected value.

Central Limit Theorem

\[\frac{\sqrt n(\overline X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)\]

Classical hypotheses: i.i.d., finite mean, finite and positive variance. It describes the shape of the fluctuations that the LLN ignores, and justifies confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

1.5 Why does √n appear?

The √n question
  1. For independent variables the variances add: \[\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\Big)=n\sigma^2\]
  2. Dividing by \(n\) divides the variance by \(n^2\): \[\operatorname{Var}(\overline X_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\]
  3. So the standard deviation (standard error) shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\): \[SE(\overline X_n)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\]
  4. The error \(\overline X_n-\mu\) tends to 0 (LLN), so its limit is degenerate. Multiplying by \(\sqrt n\) is exactly the scaling that keeps variance equal to \(\sigma^2\): a non-trivial, non-degenerate limit (CLT).
Statistical meaning: the precision of an estimate improves only as \(\sqrt n\) (4× the data → half the error), and a \(95\%\) confidence interval is \(\overline X_n\pm1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt n\).

Section 02 · Conditional Probability, Bayes' Theorem

2.1 Conditional probability

Given a sample space \(\Omega\) and events \(A,B\): the joint probability is \(P(A\cap B)\), the marginal is \(P(A)\), and the conditional restricts attention to the world where \(B\) occurred:

\[P(A|B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\quad P(B)>0\]

Events are independent if \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\), i.e. \(P(A|B)=P(A)\): learning \(B\) tells nothing about \(A\).

Example. \(P(B)=0.65\) (student attended lectures), \(P(A\cap B)=0.30\) (attended and passed). Then \(P(A|B)=0.30/0.65\approx0.46\).

2.2 Bayes' theorem

\[P(A|B)=\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)=P(B|A)P(A)+P(B|\neg A)P(\neg A)\]
ComponentMeaning
Prior \(P(A)\)Initial belief about the event
Likelihood \(P(B|A)\)Probability of the evidence given the event
Posterior \(P(A|B)\)Updated probability after seeing the evidence
Evidence \(P(B)\)Overall probability of the data
PRIOR+ data →LIKELIHOOD⇒POSTERIOR

The denominator \(P(B)\) is a normalizing constant: it makes the posteriors over all hypotheses sum (or integrate) to 1. Check: \(P(A|B)=\frac{0.85\cdot0.20}{0.25}=0.68\) with \(P(B\cap A)=0.17,\ P(B\cap\neg A)=0.08\).

2.3 Worked example · medical testing

Prevalence 1%, sensitivity 99%, specificity 95%. If a person tests positive, what is the probability of disease?

\[P(D|+)=\frac{Se\cdot Prev}{Se\cdot Prev+(1-Sp)(1-Prev)}=\frac{0.99\cdot0.01}{0.99\cdot0.01+0.05\cdot0.99}\approx16.7\%\]

Out of 1000 people: 10 are ill (≈10 true positives), 990 healthy (≈50 false positives). Most positives are false: a very accurate test does not guarantee a high posterior when the prior is small (the base-rate effect).

2.4 Bayesian statistical inference

In Bayesian inference an unknown parameter \(\theta\) is a random variable. Prior knowledge \(\pi(\theta)\) is combined with the data through the likelihood:

\[\pi(\theta|x)=\frac{L(\theta;x)\,\pi(\theta)}{\int L(\theta;x)\pi(\theta)d\theta}\ \propto\ L(\theta;x)\,\pi(\theta)\]

The posterior gives point estimates (mean, mode) and credible intervals: an interval with 95% posterior probability of containing \(\theta\), a direct probability statement, unlike a frequentist confidence interval.

Coin example. Prior \(p\sim Beta(2,2)\); data: 7 heads, 3 tails (Binomial likelihood). Conjugacy gives \(\alpha_{post}=\alpha+h,\ \beta_{post}=\beta+t\), so posterior \(Beta(9,5)\) with mean \(9/14\approx0.643\), between the prior mean 0.5 and the sample frequency 0.7.

2.5 Applications, advantages and limitations

Applications

  • Medical diagnosis
  • Spam filtering & cybersecurity (intrusion detection)
  • Machine learning
  • Reliability and risk assessment
  • Scientific inference

Advantages

  • Incorporates prior information
  • Updates beliefs as data arrive
  • Full distribution for parameters
  • Useful with little data if the prior is justified

Limitations

  • Prior choice can be subjective
  • Sensitive to prior with limited data
  • Computationally heavy in complex models
  • Wrong model ⇒ misleading conclusions
BayesianFrequentist
Parameters are random variablesParameters are fixed, unknown
Uses prior and likelihoodUses likelihood and sampling distributions
Posterior probabilities, credible intervalsConfidence intervals and hypothesis tests
Updates beliefs with new dataEvaluates procedures over repeated sampling

Note that as \(n\) grows the likelihood dominates the prior, so Bayesian and frequentist conclusions typically agree (try 700 heads / 300 tails in the explorer with different priors).

2.6 Final summary

  • Conditional probability shows how information changes event probabilities.
  • Bayes' theorem is the rule for updating them.
  • Prior, likelihood and posterior are the three components of Bayesian inference.
  • Prior choice and model assumptions can influence conclusions.
