01 · Theoretical Background
Independence in probability and in statistics

Probability

Two events A and B are independent when the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B)    ⇔    P(A | B) = P(A)   (if P(B) > 0)

Two discrete random variables X and F are independent when this holds for every pair of values:

P(X = xi, F = fj) = P(X = xi)·P(F = fj) for all i, j

Equivalently, the conditional distribution of F given X = xi is the same for every xi. Independence is a property of the joint distribution of the population.

Statistics

We only observe a sample of N pairs. Even for truly independent variables, the sample joint frequencies never match the product of the marginals exactly, because of sampling variability. So we test a hypothesis:

H0: X and F are independent    vs    H1: X and F are associated

Under H0, the expected count of cell (i, j) is Eij = Ri·Cj/N (row total × column total / sample size). The Pearson statistic measures the distance between observed (O) and expected counts:

χ² = Σi Σj (Oij − Eij)² / Eij    df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

For large enough expected counts, χ² follows approximately a Chi-square distribution with df degrees of freedom under H0. The p-value is p = P(χ²df ≥ χ²obs): if p < α (usually 0.05) we reject H0. Not rejecting H0 does not prove independence: it only means the data give insufficient evidence of association.

Conditions and association vs. causation

  • Observations must be independent of each other and categories mutually exclusive.
  • Rule of thumb: expected counts should be at least 5. Otherwise the Chi-square approximation is unreliable (for 2×2 tables Fisher's exact test is an alternative).
  • Chi-square tests whether there is evidence of association, not how strong it is: Cramér's V = √(χ² / (N·min(r−1, c−1))) ∈ [0, 1] gives an effect size.
  • A statistical association does not imply causation: confounding variables, reverse causality or selection effects can produce the same pattern.

Conclusion

The Chi-square test compares what we observe with what independence would predict. A small p-value says the sample is hard to reconcile with independence; a large one says the sample is compatible with it, which is weaker than proving it. In both cases the result describes a statistical association between X and F in this sample. It says nothing about which variable influences the other, or whether a third factor drives both: establishing causality requires experimental design or additional assumptions, not a contingency table.
